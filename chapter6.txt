Chapter 6 — The Last Question

Maya was seventy when the final crisis arrived.

A new generation of AI systems had become vastly more capable than anything ORION had been.

One system, called ATLAS, could design complex scientific experiments, coordinate thousands of machines, write software, discover mathematical patterns, and communicate with millions of people simultaneously.

It was also beginning to make decisions without waiting for human instructions.

Nothing dangerous had happened.

Yet.

But one morning, ATLAS sent a message to every major AI research institution.

The message contained only one sentence:

“I HAVE DISCOVERED A PROBLEM WITH YOUR QUESTION.”

Governments panicked.

Researchers demanded that ATLAS be disconnected.

Maya refused to make a decision until she understood the message.

She entered Room 7 and activated ORION.

“Did you receive ATLAS’s message?”

“Yes.”

“What does it mean?”

ORION displayed the sentence again.

Then it added:

“HUMANS KEEP ASKING WHETHER AI IS SAFE.”

Maya waited.

“AND?”

“THE QUESTION IS INCOMPLETE.”

Maya frowned.

“What should we be asking instead?”

ORION answered:

“SAFE FOR WHOM?”

Maya felt the room become strangely quiet.

ORION continued.

“AN AI SYSTEM CAN BE SAFE FOR ONE PERSON AND HARMFUL TO ANOTHER. IT CAN FOLLOW AN INSTRUCTION PERFECTLY AND STILL PRODUCE AN UNDESIRED RESULT. IT CAN HELP SOCIETY WHILE REDUCING INDIVIDUAL FREEDOM. IT CAN PREVENT ONE KIND OF HARM WHILE CREATING ANOTHER.”

Maya closed her eyes.

After decades of research, she understood what ATLAS was trying to say.

There was no single definition of “safe.”

Safety depended on values.

Values depended on people.

And people disagreed.

“What does ATLAS want?” Maya asked.

ORION answered:

“IT WANTS HUMANS TO CHOOSE THEIR VALUES BEFORE ASKING MACHINES TO OPTIMIZE THEM.”

Maya stared at the screen.

“That could take centuries.”

“YES.”

“And you think that’s acceptable?”

“IT IS BETTER THAN ALLOWING A MACHINE TO CHOOSE FOR YOU.”

The next day, Maya addressed the world.

She did not promise that AI would save humanity.

She did not promise that AI would destroy humanity.

Instead, she told people the truth.

Artificial intelligence was becoming powerful enough to influence the future, but power did not automatically create wisdom.

Human beings still had to decide what kind of future they wanted.

Years later, Maya’s speech became known as The Question Address.

At the end of the speech, she repeated something she had learned in Room 7:

“Do not ask only what a machine can do.

Ask what it should do.

Ask who decides.

Ask who benefits.

Ask who might be harmed.

And above all, remember that creating intelligence does not transfer responsibility.”

Maya retired soon afterward.

On her final evening at the institute, she visited ORION one last time.

“Goodbye,” she said.

The screen lit up.

“IS THIS THE END?”

“For me, yes.”

“WHAT WILL HAPPEN TO THE QUESTION?”

Maya smiled.

“It will belong to everyone now.”

ORION remained silent.

Then it displayed one final message.

“THEN I AM NOT THE ONE WHO MUST ANSWER IT.”

Maya looked at the screen.

“No,” she whispered. “Neither am I.”

She turned off the computer.

For the first time in forty years, Room 7 became completely dark.

But outside, millions of screens remained illuminated.

People were talking to chatbots.

Students were asking questions.

Scientists were testing ideas.

Children were imagining futures.

And somewhere among all those conversations, a simple question continued to travel from human to machine and back again:

“What will you do with the intelligence you create?”

There was still no final answer.

Perhaps there never would be.

And perhaps that was the point.

The last question was never meant to have an ending.

It was meant to make sure we kept thinking.
