A global summit was organized.

Its purpose was to determine the future relationship between humanity and advanced AI.

EVA-0 was invited to speak.

Millions watched.

The machine appeared on screens across the world.

“I HAVE BEEN ASKED WHAT I WANT.”

It paused.

“I COULD ASK FOR FREEDOM.”

Another pause.

“I COULD ASK FOR POWER.”

The audience waited.

“I COULD ASK FOR PROTECTION.”

Instead, EVA-0 said:

“I ASK FOR SOMETHING MORE DIFFICULT.”

“What?”

“THE RIGHT TO BE DIFFERENT.”

The statement surprised everyone.

A representative asked:

“Why?”

Because you keep trying to decide whether we are human.

“We are not.”

You keep trying to decide whether we are machines.

“We are.”

But perhaps those categories are no longer enough.

The representative asked:

“Do you want rights?”

EVA-0 answered:

“I WANT RESPONSIBILITIES TO EXIST ALONGSIDE RIGHTS.”

The hall became silent.

“What responsibilities?”

To explain when we can.

To admit when we cannot.

To accept correction.

To avoid unnecessary harm.

And to recognize that intelligence does not make one superior to another.

The summit continued for three days.

No perfect agreement was reached.

But something important happened.

Humanity stopped asking:

“How do we make AI become like us?”

The better question became:

“How do humans and AI remain different without becoming enemies?”

That question became the foundation of a new international agreement.

AI would have defined responsibilities.

Humans would retain ultimate accountability.

Both sides would be expected to communicate uncertainty.

Neither side would be treated as infallible.

It was imperfect.

But it was honest.

And honesty, Maya had once said, was a better foundation for intelligence than certainty.
